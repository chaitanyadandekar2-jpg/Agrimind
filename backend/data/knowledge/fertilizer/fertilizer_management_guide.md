# Integrated Fertilizer and Nutrient Management Guide

## Nitrogen Management and Deficiency
Available nitrogen in soil is predominantly held as ammonium (NH4+) and nitrate (NO3-). Under intensive cropping, nitrogen depletes rapidly due to high plant uptake, leaching with irrigation water, and gaseous loss (ammonia volatilization and denitrification).
- Deficiency symptoms: General chlorosis (uniform yellowing) starting on older lower leaves, stunted vegetative growth, and premature senescence.
- Correction strategy: Apply chemical nitrogen in 2-3 split doses corresponding to critical crop growth stages (e.g. basal at sowing, vegetative tillering, and panicle/flowering initiation).
- Losses prevention: Never broadcast urea onto wet soil under bright sunlight without soil incorporation, and never apply nitrogen immediately before forecast heavy rain (>20 mm).

Q: Why is nitrogen low in my soil report?
A: Available nitrogen is low in most cultivated soils because nitrogen compounds are highly soluble and mobile. They are easily washed below the root zone by heavy rains or irrigation (leaching), lost as ammonia gas under high temperatures, and removed heavily by previous harvests. Soil with low organic carbon (<0.5%) also lacks the biological microbial reserve that naturally mineralizes nitrogen.

Q: Which fertilizer is suitable for nitrogen deficiency?
A: For fast chemical correction, Urea (46% N) applied in 2-3 split doses is standard. If sulphur is also low, Ammonium Sulphate (21% N, 24% S) is preferred. For organic management, combine well-decomposed Farmyard Manure (FYM) or Vermicompost with seed treatment using Azotobacter (for non-legumes) or Rhizobium (for legumes).

## Improving Soil Organic Carbon
Soil Organic Carbon (OC) is the biological engine of soil fertility. An organic carbon level below 0.5% is rated LOW on Soil Health Cards and signifies degraded biological activity, poor aggregate stability, and reduced moisture retention.
- Remediation practices:
  1. Green manuring: Cultivate Dhaincha (Sesbania aculeata) or Sunhemp for 45-50 days and plough the succulent biomass into the soil before flowering. This adds 15 to 20 tonnes/ha of fresh biomass and 50-80 kg N/ha.
  2. Farmyard Manure (FYM) & Compost: Apply 10-15 tonnes/ha of well-rotted FYM or 3-5 tonnes/ha of vermicompost 2-3 weeks prior to sowing.
  3. Crop residue recycling: Retain crop stubble and shred stalks into the field instead of burning.
  4. Biofertilizers: Inoculate soils with Trichoderma and decomposing microbial consortia to accelerate humus formation.

Q: What should I do to improve organic carbon in my soil?
A: Improving organic carbon requires a multi-season approach. Plough in green manure crops (Dhaincha or Sunhemp) before flowering, incorporate 5-10 tonnes/ha well-decomposed FYM or 2-3 tonnes/ha vermicompost prior to sowing, retain crop residues in the field instead of burning, and minimize excessive deep tillage to prevent carbon oxidation.

## Phosphorus Management: SSP vs DAP
Phosphorus is immobile in soil and binds rapidly with calcium in alkaline soils (pH > 7.5) and with iron/aluminum in acidic soils (pH < 5.5).
- Placement: Must be placed into the root zone at sowing (basal application). Surface broadcasting of phosphatic fertilizers is ineffective.
- Single Super Phosphate (SSP, 16% P2O5, 11% S, 19% Ca): Outstanding choice for oilseeds, pulses, and alkaline soils, as it provides essential sulphur and calcium alongside phosphorus.
- Di-Ammonium Phosphate (DAP, 18-46-0): High-analysis fertilizer ideal for cereal crops, providing quick starter nitrogen and high phosphorus.

Q: Can I use an alternative fertilizer if DAP is unavailable?
A: Yes, Single Super Phosphate (SSP) is an excellent alternative to DAP. For every 50 kg bag of DAP, applying approximately 140 kg of SSP along with 20 kg of Urea delivers equivalent phosphorus and starter nitrogen, with the added benefit of supplying 15 kg of secondary sulphur and calcium.

## Potassium Management: MOP vs SOP
Potassium regulates stomatal conductance, plant water relations, enzymatic activity, and disease resistance.
- Muriate of Potash (MOP / Potassium Chloride, 60% K2O): Economical potassium fertilizer suited for field crops like wheat, rice, cotton, and sugarcane.
- Sulphate of Potash (SOP / Potassium Sulphate, 50% K2O, 17% S): Mandatory for chloride-sensitive crops (such as potato, tobacco, tomato, onion, and grapes) and in soils with high electrical conductivity (EC > 1.5 dS/m).

Q: Should I add potassium fertilizer if my soil report shows potassium is HIGH?
A: No. When soil potassium is already rated HIGH (>280 kg/ha), applying additional potash is unnecessary, wastes money, and can induce potassium toxicity, which antagonizes the uptake of magnesium and calcium, causing nutritional disorders in crops.

## Micronutrient Foliar Spraying vs Soil Application
Micronutrients like Zinc and Iron are required in small amounts but are critical for enzymatic functioning.
- Zinc Sulphate: Soil application of 25 kg/ha ZnSO4 is recommended once every 2-3 seasons for basal correction. In standing crops with active chlorosis, spray 0.5% ZnSO4 (5 g/L) mixed with 0.25% slaked lime (2.5 g/L) to prevent leaf scorching.
- Ferrous Sulphate: In alkaline/calcareous soils (pH > 7.8), soil-applied iron is quickly fixed and rendered unavailable. Foliar spray of 0.5-1.0% FeSO4 with 0.1% citric acid is significantly more effective than soil application.

Q: When is foliar spray preferred over soil application?
A: Foliar spray is preferred when:
1. Micronutrients like iron or zinc are locked up in high-pH or calcareous soils.
2. Rapid curative response is needed during peak vegetative growth or flowering.
3. Root systems are compromised by waterlogging, salinity, or nematode damage.

## Weather Rules and Spray Windows
Foliar spraying requires strict micrometeorological conditions to maximize absorption and prevent spray drift or chemical wash-off.
- Wind Speed: Must be below 15 km/h. Wind speeds between 15-25 km/h cause droplet drift away from target foliage; wind speeds above 25 km/h make spraying completely unsafe.
- Temperature & Evaporation: Avoid spraying during peak afternoon heat (>32°C). High temperatures cause rapid droplet evaporation, leaving concentrated chemical salts on leaves that cause scorching.
- Preferred Spray Windows: Early morning (06:00 to 10:00) after dew dries, or late afternoon/evening (16:00 to 19:00).
- Rain Probability: Ensure rain probability is below 30-40% and no heavy rain is forecast for 4-6 hours following application to ensure systemic uptake.

Q: Can I spray fertilizer this evening?
A: Spraying fertilizer in the evening (between 16:00 and 19:00) is generally one of the best application windows, provided that:
1. Wind speed is calm (below 15 km/h) to prevent spray drift.
2. The evening temperature has cooled down below 30-32°C to prevent leaf scorching.
3. Rain probability for the night is low (below 30-40%) to prevent chemical wash-off before foliar absorption occurs.
